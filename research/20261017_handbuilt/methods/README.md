# Interpretability methods tested on a network with a known mechanism

Framing: this is a replication of known lessons on a new kind of test network, a recurrent network built on exact cancellation and a rotating timer, plus one new calibration (of the "reproduces the mistakes" test) and an interchange test that confirms the ARC account by intervention. Related work, checked against the primary sources on 2026-10-17 (`../citations.md`): ablations show necessity rather than role (Morcos et al. 2018, single-direction ablations); zero, mean and resample ablation can disagree and resampling is usually preferred (Heimersheim and Nanda's activation-patching guide, 2024; optimal-ablation work by Li and Janson, 2024; causal scrubbing, Redwood 2022); mechanisms found on templated or simplified inputs may not hold on real ones (interpretability illusions, Bolukbasi et al. 2021); ground-truth testing of methods with compiled or planted circuits (Tracr, Lindner et al. 2023; InterpBench, Gupta et al. 2024).

Started 2026-10-17. Resample ablation was run alongside zero and mean ablation (third column of `ablation.log`); in the hand-built network it scores close to zero ablation, because resampling one timer copy from another list breaks the cancellation with the other copy. The hand-built 16-neuron network (`algzoo.handbuilt.build_v2`, gains 100, 98.0% accurate) has a mechanism we know exactly because we set every weight. We ran on it the methods we had used on ARC's trained model, and checked each against the planted truth. Logs are in this folder; scripts are in `../scripts/methods_*.py`.

## Findings

- **Single-neuron ablation says "everything matters" in a circuit whose parts cancel.** Zeroing any one of the nine timer and vote neurons drops the hand-built network to about 10% accuracy, although their jobs differ (one timer copy, the other copy, the vote register). Ablation importance does not separate roles. Split by answer position, zero ablation did recover one planted role exactly: without the "last input" neuron the network never answers position 9 correctly but still gets 98% of positions 0–8 (`ablation.log`).
- **The type of ablation changes the importance ranking.** Rarely-firing detector neurons are hurt more by mean ablation than by zero ablation (8% against 18% accuracy in the hand-built network); stores are hurt less. In ARC's model, neuron 13 ranks below neuron 15 under zero ablation (30% against 64%) and above it under mean ablation (61% against 53%). For ARC's neuron 3, zero ablation of its final output drops positions 1 and 6 to 6–7%, but resampling it from another list gives 50–56%: neuron 3 is usually on, so zero is the larger departure. Our earlier reading leaned on zero ablation and overstated its role.
- **"Two groups' pushes are mirror images" is a tautology when the total is invisible.** In the hand-built network every random split of the neurons gives exact mirror images (correlation −1.00), just like the designed pair of timer copies (`split_tautology.log`). What singles out the designed pair is an intervention: a bigger number wipes one copy and reveals a vote. (An earlier explainer wrongly presented the mirror image as evidence.)
- **Reproducing few mistakes does not tell an incomplete account from a complete one.** Our additive vote model, fitted to a network's choices, reproduced only 10–29% of ARC's mistakes, and we had concluded that ARC's mistakes come from interactions the account misses. On the hand-built network, whose mechanism is complete and known, the same model agrees with 97% of answers but reproduces only 3–14% of its mistakes (`additive_calibration.log`). Mistakes are set by fine details, such as exactly where a gate switches, that a vote summary does not encode, even when the mechanism is fully understood. A further check makes this sharper: the hand-built network's own exact mechanism, with only its gate sharpness changed (gain 300 or 1000 instead of 100), reproduces just 11–21% of the gain-100 network's mistakes. About half of the hand-built network's mistakes are lists with at most one positive number, which a vote model without a default cannot express; this makes the comparison with ARC's model (18% such mistakes) uneven.
- **Probes on simplified inputs cannot tell different mechanisms apart.** Run 3's two-spike mechanism checks pass identically on both networks, although they handle smaller numbers in opposite ways (one erasable vote against several competing votes).

- **Hypothesis-driven interchange tests work, if they patch the right neurons at the right step** (`interchange.log`, `../scripts/methods_interchange.py`). In the hand-built network, copying the timer copies from a donor list (where the to-be-revealed number sat elsewhere) moves the answer to the donor's position in 168 of 168 lists when both numbers arrived at least two steps before the bigger one, but in only 6 of 72 when one arrived just one step before: the information is then still on its way through the gate neurons. Copying copy A alone works as well as both, and copy B alone does nothing, because B is rebuilt from A at every step; we had expected the opposite. So where a variable lives changes from step to step, and the test showed a dependency we had not thought about, in a network we designed.
- **Our single-step tool recovers a planted role.** Holding the hand-built network's new-second gate at zero one step after a later smaller number arrives removes that number's vote in 33 of 36 lists. This supports the same kind of test used on ARC's neuron 5.
- **Applied to ARC's model, the step-0 interchange test passes by construction** (correction, 2026-10-17; `../scripts/check_olah_points.py`). At the step the bigger number arrives, neurons 1, 6, 7 and 8 are zero in both lists (all two-number lists; 90% of random lists), so copying the other twelve copies the whole state and must move the answer (100%); copying the four copies zeros onto zeros (0%). That result says nothing about the account. Informative replacements: (a) zeroing the four by hand with no bigger number makes a lone number's position win in 91% of placements (random four: 2%), and restoring them at the bigger number's step leaves the network right in 18% (random four: 96%) (`artificial_wipe.log`); (b) one to four steps after the wipe, when the four are active again, copying the twelve still moves the answer in 83–90% of two-number lists and about 90% of random lists, and copying the four never does (`arc_interchange_later.log`, `arc_interchange_random.log`). The hand-built test above patches the step before the wipe, so it is not affected. Lesson: before an interchange test, check whether the patched group is the only part of the state that differs between the two runs.

## Caveats from review

Several of these points are known in the field: ablation measures necessity rather than role, mean ablation misleads for units far from their mean, mechanisms found on templated inputs may not hold on real ones ("interpretability illusions"), and ground-truth testing of methods has been done with compiled transformers (Tracr, InterpBench). What is new here is the kind of test network: a recurrent network built on exact cancellation and a rotating timer. Also: the hand-built network is far more fragile than a trained one (0.1% weight noise drops it to 44%; ARC's model keeps 92% at 0.5%), so its ablation results may not carry over to trained networks; and single neurons are the wrong unit for its 3-neuron timer copies. ## Lessons, so far from one test network

Each method above answered a narrower question than the one we asked of it. Ablation measures necessity, not role. Mirror-image pushes measure invisibility of a total, not the existence of copies. A summary model's failure to reproduce mistakes measures how fine-grained the decision is, not whether the mechanism is incomplete. Testing methods on a network built to a known design was cheap here and caught two of our own over-interpretations.
