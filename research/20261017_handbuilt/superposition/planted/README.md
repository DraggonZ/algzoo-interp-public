# Planted test: can sparse autoencoders recover roles we built in? (2026-10-19)

Answer: no. On the hand-built networks, whose roles are known by construction, the same sparse autoencoder (SAE) recipe that failed on ARC's trained network also fails to recover the roles. It reconstructs the state well (as little as 0.2% of the variance unexplained for v21 and 0.01% for v19), but its latents mostly do not line up with the built roles. Splicing breaks both networks, and this happens even for the few roles that are recovered. The recipe itself works: on a toy with genuinely sparse planted features, it recovers 26 of 28 feature directions. The failure comes from the networks, not the code. Their roles are dense rather than sparse: a typical v21 state has 17 of its 28 neurons on, and v19 has 13 of 16. Sparse dictionary learning assumes few active features per state, so it has no reason to prefer the built basis.

What was tested. Three setups, with the recipe of `../sae/train_sae.py`: TopK SAEs, each neuron scaled to unit standard deviation, all ten steps pooled, widths 1x, 2x and 4x the input dimension, and k 2, 4 and 8. The first two setups use v21's own 28-neuron state and v19's own 16-neuron state (with kappa 0.5). The third is the classic superposition setup: a fixed random 16 x 28 projection of v21's scaled state, so 28 roles must be found in 16 dimensions. The ground truth is each network's own neurons. Rotating groups have no preferred basis, so their truth is a three-dimensional subspace: v21's four clock and reveal triples, and v19's clock triple. The memory delay line is always on and so runs in the linear regime. It is scored both per slot and as one subspace.

How roles are matched. As in `../sae/eval_roles_fair.py`, the part of every role and every latent that is a linear function of shared quantities is removed first. Those quantities are the largest so far, the current number and the step, plus (new here) the largest so far before the current number. Without that addition, v19's memory slots share one component, the latest jump, which is over 99% of their remaining variance. Any latent carrying that jump would then "match" all eight slots. This was a judgement call. It makes the numbers here stricter than ARC's, so they are not exactly comparable. After the removal, the store and the detector are pure functions of the shared quantities, so they count only in the raw score. Per neuron: the best |correlation| with any single latent. Per subspace: as many latents as the group has neurons are picked greedily, then we take the smallest canonical correlation. A value of 1 means those latents span the whole subspace.

## Results

| setup | unexplained variance (held out) | neurons matched above 0.9 (shared part removed) | subspaces recovered above 0.9 | spliced, last step / every step |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| reference: random rotation of v21's state | | 0 of 26, mean 0.74 | none | |
| reference: ARC (`../sae/`, grid lists) | 1.9-9.1% | v21 roles: 3-4 of 28, mean 0.70-0.73 | | 33-61% / 22-33% |

Exact figures for every width and k are in `eval.log`.

v21: the eight matches are not eight roles. Seven are the reveal group (QC0-2, QE0-2 and QF). These neurons are silent except at records, so after the shared part is removed they are almost one signal. A single latent matches all seven at 0.99. The subspace score shows the reveal group's real structure is missed (0.2-0.6). The eighth match is Zc, the "two steps after a record" pulse. Like the reveal group, it is sparse, which is the property the method needs. The clocks, the memory slots, the keep gate and the flag are matched at only 0.3-0.8. Wider SAEs and larger k do not change this. With all latents together, a linear map reads the clock subspaces back exactly (canonical correlation 1.00), but no small set of latents spans them. So the information is in the dictionary, spread across many latents, and not organised by role.

v19: worse than chance. SAE latents match v19's roles worse than a random rotation does (mean best 0.13-0.60 against 0.62) and worse than the principal components (0.79). The reason is scale. Most of each clock neuron is the level 10^4 (m1 - x). The phase that carries the algorithm is about 10^-4 of that neuron's spread, and so about 10^-8 of its variance. An SAE judged by squared error has no reason to represent it. The same holds, less extremely, for the memory's votes, which ride on a level 40 times larger.

Projected v21 (superposition): 16 random dimensions are enough to read each of v21's roles back linearly. The mean multiple correlation is 0.97, and 25 of 26 roles are above 0.9. That is because the roles' specific parts are strongly correlated with each other. The SAE latents do no better than in the unprojected case: the same 8 of 26 (the reveal group and Zc), and no subspace recovered. Compressing to fewer dimensions than roles is therefore not what defeats the method. It fails the same way with 28 dimensions as with 16.

Splicing fails even where roles are recovered. On v21, the best last-step splice gives 52% (4x wide, k 4) against the network's 96%. Splicing at every step gives at most 18%. Noise of the same per-neuron size as the SAE's error does about as badly: 32-48% at the last step and 17-21% at every step. So the failure is set by the size of the error, not by which roles are recovered. v21 is called robust, yet even noise of 0.1% of each neuron's spread at the last step drops it to 87%. v19 falls to chance (about 10%) under any SAE and under 0.1% noise. These numbers are on random lists. ARC's were on the scoring grid, so the two sets of percentages are close but not identical in meaning.

## Controls (`extra.log`)

The roles are dense. In v21, a mean of 14.5 neurons are active per state (median 17, and 17 from step 3 on). In v19 the mean is 11.3 (median 13). With k = 2-8, a TopK SAE cannot use the neuron basis at all. Raising k to 12, 16 or 20 (1x wide) cuts unexplained variance to 0.04-0.15%. Recovery barely changes: 8-9 of 26 neurons above 0.9 for v21, and 1 of 14 for v19. At k close to the dimension, any basis reconstructs, and nothing singles out the built one.

The recipe works when its assumption holds. In the toy check, 28 random unit directions in 16 dimensions are each active 8% of the time with exponential magnitude. A 2x-wide SAE with k 2 finds 26 of 28 directions with |cosine| above 0.9, and its activations match 21 of 28 at |correlation| above 0.9 (23 of 28 at k 4).

## Reading

This is an interpretation. The ARC result ("SAE latents match the hand-built roles no better than ARC's own neurons") cannot be read as evidence that ARC lacks those roles. The same method fails to find them where they are certainly present. Two properties of these networks defeat it, and both are likely shared by ARC. First, the roles are dense, with most neurons on most of the time on top of large levels. Second, the computation lives in directions with tiny variance (the clock phase), which a squared-error objective ignores. A role-finding method for this setting would need a different objective, for example one that weights directions by their effect on the scores, or a different notion of feature than "sparse direction in the state". Caveats: one seed per setting, the SAE recipe has no dead-latent revival (up to 45 of 64 latents dead at k 2), and matching is by correlation, not by causal tests.

## Files

`common_planted.py` holds the networks, role groups, projection and shared quantities. `train.py` and `train.log` train the 27 SAEs (the `saes_*.pt` files). `eval.py` and `eval.log` cover role recovery, subspaces and splicing; `eval_v19.log` is an earlier v19-only run that used the original set of shared quantities, kept to show the inflated memory matches. `extra.py` and `extra.log` hold the sparsity count, the larger-k runs and the toy check. Run with `OMP_NUM_THREADS=4 PYTHONPATH=/data/output/src/algzoo/src /opt/venv/bin/python <script>` from this folder. Each script takes a few minutes or less on the GPU.
