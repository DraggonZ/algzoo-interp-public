How a 16-neuron network finds the second-largest number

ARC's AlgZoo model reads ten random numbers one at a time and must point at the position of the second-largest. It has 16 ReLU neurons and no biases, and gets 95.3% of random inputs right. This page runs the real network in your browser. Drag the bars to change the inputs, step through time, and switch neurons off to see what breaks. The explanations come from our reverse-engineering; where it is uncertain, the text says so.

Examples:

The input

Ten numbers, read left to right. Drag a bar up or down. Orange marks the largest, green the second-largest (the right answer).

What happened at each step

    Scores

    The network's ten output scores, read from its state now (minus their average). Only the scores after the last step are the real answer; earlier ones show what it would say if the sequence ended here. Votes move one position left each step.

    The hidden clock

    Ten of the neurons, seen in the plane where a single input makes them turn about 40° per step (faint loop: a lone input of 1). Any positive input starts the clock. The output layer cannot see it: two copies of it cancel at the output, so the largest number casts no vote. When a new largest arrives it wipes one copy, and the other shows where the old largest arrived.

    Running maximum

    Neuron 4 fires only when a number beats the largest so far. Neuron 2 adds up those jumps, so it holds about 17× the largest (it starts to forget after about four steps).

    largest so far (true)neuron 2 ÷ 17

    Who gets a lasting vote: neuron 5

    Each dot is a positive number, placed by its size relative to the second-largest so far when it arrived. Neuron 5 fires one step later for numbers above about 0.8 of the second (filled dots), and those keep a lasting vote. Below that, the number is forgotten after one step. New largest numbers are drawn at the top.

    Close calls: change one number and watch the answer

    Pick a position. The blue line is the network's answer as that number goes from −3 to 3 with the rest held fixed; the grey line is the right answer. Where they part, the network errs. Most mistakes are near-ties between the second and third. Which side wins there differs from model to model and is set by fine detail in the weights, so no short rule predicts it.

    Position:

    What we know, and what we don't

    • Known: a running maximum (neurons 2 and 4); a hidden clock in two cancelling copies; a new largest wipes the lowering neurons (1, 6, 7, 8) and reveals the old largest's position; neuron 5 decides which numbers keep a lasting vote; votes slide one position per step; several lasting votes can coexist and the biggest usually wins; neuron 3 votes for positions 1 and 6 and helps the clock at positions 0 and 2–5.
    • Not known: why neuron 5's cut sits near 0.8 of the second (it looks like a hedge, since the network knows the second only roughly); why the clock needs neuron 3 at those positions; exactly how votes interact. A model built from independent votes matches the network's answers 95% of the time but only about a quarter of its mistakes.
